How this was built, and what did the work

The specification, the sector structure, the scenario definitions and every judgment in the Assumptions Register are the author’s. The code was written with an AI coding assistant: the ingestion layer, the transition model, the Monte Carlo runner, the tests and this site were drafted and revised in conversation with a tool, then run, read and corrected by the author. Every commit in the working repository is authored by a human, and nothing about that is hidden here.

What that tool did not do, and what matters for a reader: it produced no number. There is no model inference, no retrieval-augmented claim, and no LLM opinion anywhere in the pipeline. A probability on this site is the output of transition_model.py run 10,000 times over model_params.yaml, and the arithmetic is deterministic: same parameters, same baseline, same seed, same table. The exclusion in About the study - “no opinion aggregation, no LLM panels” - is about where a probability may come from, and it still holds: the assistant was asked to write and check code, never what it thinks will happen.

So the honest summary of the method is: human judgment about the structure, machine help with the typing, arithmetic doing the rest.

Standing of this work, stated plainly

There is no peer review behind it, no institutional endorsement, no funding statement, and no dataset of our own: the empirical content is other people’s published statistics, re-read. The model’s arithmetic is deliberately simple and fully exposed, because a scenario study earns attention by being falsifiable, not by being sophisticated. Its useful output is the Assumptions Register plus the sensitivity table: those two tell a reader which claims would survive an argument, and which are one threshold wide.

If you are going to cite it, cite it as CITATION.cff specifies, and quote a probability together with its model_params_hash, model_code_hash, seed and N. Those four tokens are the difference between a citation and a rumour.
